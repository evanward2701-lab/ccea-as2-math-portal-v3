## 6. Big Picture Explanation

Integration at AS level was mostly about reversing simple polynomial differentiation to find areas under curves. At A2 level, integration becomes a vast, interconnected toolkit. Because the Product Rule and Chain Rule make differentiation complex, reversing those processes requires specific, targeted strategies. 

You will learn to look at an integral and diagnose it: Is it a standard result? Does it need a trigonometric identity? Is it a reverse chain rule? Do I need substitution, or integration by parts? 

Mastering this topic is like learning to play chess; you must look several moves ahead to choose the right technique. Ultimately, these tools allow us to solve **Differential Equations**—the mathematical language used to model everything from population growth and radioactive decay to the cooling of a cup of tea.

## 7. Key Definitions and Notation

*   **Integrand:** The function being integrated. In $\int f(x) \, dx$, $f(x)$ is the integrand.
*   **Definite Integral:** An integral with upper and lower limits, e.g., $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx$, which evaluates to a specific value (often an area).
*   **Indefinite Integral:** An integral without limits, which evaluates to a family of functions and must include a constant of integration, $+ c$.
*   **Differential Equation:** An equation that contains a derivative, such as $\frac{dy}{dx} = xy + y$. Solving it means finding the original function $y = f(x)$.
*   **General Solution:** The solution to a differential equation that includes an unknown constant (e.g., $+ c$ or $A$).
*   **Particular Solution:** The specific solution to a differential equation found by substituting in known boundary conditions (e.g., $x=0, y=4$) to find the exact value of the constant.

## 8. Core Theory

### 8.1 The Integration Strategy Flowchart

When faced with an integration problem, you should not guess randomly. Follow a strict hierarchy of checks.

[VISUAL PLACEHOLDER: A21IntegrationMMD-001 | Source: Transcript & PDF Cheat Sheet | Insert from A21_integration_mermaid.md | Purpose: Flowchart showing the decision-making process for integration techniques]

1.  **Standard Result:** Is it in the formula book or a known derivative? (e.g., $\sec^2 x \rightarrow \tan x$). Does it just need linear scaling?
2.  **Manipulation:** Can I expand brackets or use a trig identity?
3.  **Reverse Chain Rule:** Is the numerator the derivative of the denominator? Is one factor the derivative of the "inside" of the other factor?
4.  **Fractions:** Can I split the numerator? Do I need partial fractions? Is it top-heavy (algebraic division)?
5.  **Products:** Use Integration by Parts.
6.  **Last Resort:** Substitution (usually given in the exam).

---

### 8.2 Standard Results and Scaling

You must memorise the standard results that come directly from reversing differentiation. 

**Key Standard Integrals:**
*   $\int e^x \, dx = e^x + c$
*   $\int \frac{1}{x} \, dx = \ln|x| + c$
*   $\int \cos x \, dx = \sin x + c$
*   $\int \sin x \, dx = -\cos x + c$
*   $\int \sec^2 x \, dx = \tan x + c$
*   $\int \sec x \tan x \, dx = \sec x + c$
*   $\int \cosec x \cot x \, dx = -\cosec x + c$
*   $\int \cosec^2 x \, dx = -\cot x + c$

**Scaling Rule for $f(ax+b)$:**
If you know the integral of $f(x)$, then for any linear inner function $ax+b$, you integrate as normal and divide by the coefficient of $x$ (which is $a$).

$$ \int f'(ax+b) \, dx = \frac{1}{a}f(ax+b) + c $$

**Example:**
$$ \int e^{3x+1} \, dx = \frac{1}{3}e^{3x+1} + c $$
$$ \int \cos(1 - 5x) \, dx = -\frac{1}{5}\sin(1 - 5x) + c $$

*Warning:* This scaling rule **only** works for linear inner functions ($ax+b$). You cannot use this rule for $\int e^{x^2} \, dx$.

---

### 8.3 Integrating using Trigonometric Identities

Some expressions, such as $\sin^2 x$, $\cos^2 x$, and $\tan^2 x$, cannot be integrated directly. You must use trigonometric identities to replace them with expressions you *can* integrate.

**Integrating $\sin^2 x$ and $\cos^2 x$:**
Always use the rearranged double angle formulae for $\cos 2x$:
*   $\cos 2x = 1 - 2\sin^2 x \implies \sin^2 x = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\cos 2x$
*   $\cos 2x = 2\cos^2 x - 1 \implies \cos^2 x = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\cos 2x$

**Example:** Integrate $\int \sin^2 x \, dx$
$$ \int \sin^2 x \, dx = \int \left( \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\cos 2x \right) \, dx $$
$$ = \frac{1}{2}x - \frac{1}{4}\sin 2x + c $$

**Integrating $\tan^2 x$:**
Use the Pythagorean identity: $1 + \tan^2 x \equiv \sec^2 x$.
$$ \int \tan^2 x \, dx = \int (\sec^2 x - 1) \, dx = \tan x - x + c $$

---

### 8.4 The Reverse Chain Rule (Integration by Inspection)

This is arguably the most important and frequently used technique in A2 Integration. If an expression looks like the result of the Chain Rule, you can integrate it by "considering" the original function and "scaling" it.

**Pattern 1: The numerator is the derivative of the denominator**
If a fraction has the form $\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}$, it integrates to $\ln|f(x)|$.

**Example:** $\int \frac{2x}{x^2 + 1} \, dx$
*   **Consider:** The derivative of $x^2 + 1$ is $2x$.
*   **Result:** $\ln|x^2 + 1| + c$. No scaling required.

**Example:** $\int \frac{x^2}{x^3 + 1} \, dx$
*   **Consider:** The derivative of $x^3 + 1$ is $3x^2$. We only have $x^2$.
*   **Scale:** We need $\frac{1}{3}$ of the result.
*   **Result:** $\frac{1}{3}\ln|x^3 + 1| + c$.

**Pattern 2: One factor is the derivative of the "inside" of the other**
Form: $\int k \cdot f'(x)[f(x)]^n \, dx \rightarrow \text{Try } [f(x)]^{n+1}$

**Example:** $\int x(x^2 + 5)^3 \, dx$
*   **Consider:** The $x$ outside arose from differentiating the $x^2$ inside. Consider $y = (x^2 + 5)^4$.
*   **Differentiate:** $\frac{dy}{dx} = 4(x^2 + 5)^3 \times 2x = 8x(x^2 + 5)^3$.
*   **Scale:** We want $x(x^2 + 5)^3$, not $8x$. So we multiply by $\frac{1}{8}$.
*   **Result:** $\frac{1}{8}(x^2 + 5)^4 + c$.

**Pattern 3: Trigonometric Reverse Chain Rule**
**Example:** $\int \cos x \sin^2 x \, dx$
*   **Consider:** The $\cos x$ arose from differentiating $\sin x$. Consider $y = \sin^3 x$.
*   **Differentiate:** $\frac{dy}{dx} = 3\sin^2 x \cos x$.
*   **Scale:** We want $1$, not $3$. Multiply by $\frac{1}{3}$.
*   **Result:** $\frac{1}{3}\sin^3 x + c$.

*Exam Tip:* When integrating $\sec^n x \tan x$, remember that differentiating $\sec x$ gives $\sec x \tan x$. Therefore, the power of $\sec x$ does *not* increase. 
$\int \sec^4 x \tan x \, dx = \frac{1}{4}\sec^4 x + c$.

---

### 8.5 Integration by Substitution

When the reverse chain rule doesn't work (e.g., $\int x\sqrt{2x+5} \, dx$), we use substitution to turn the integral into a simpler equivalent in terms of a new variable, $u$.

**The 4 Steps of Substitution:**
1.  **Differentiate the substitution:** Find $\frac{du}{dx}$ and rearrange to make $dx$ the subject.
2.  **Substitute:** Replace all $x$ terms and $dx$ with $u$ terms and $du$. (If it is a definite integral, you **must** change the limits from $x$-values to $u$-values).
3.  **Integrate:** Integrate the simplified expression with respect to $u$.
4.  **Replace:** If it is an indefinite integral, substitute the $x$ expression back in for $u$.

**Example:** Find $\int x\sqrt{2x + 5} \, dx$ using $u = 2x + 5$.
1.  $u = 2x + 5 \implies x = \frac{u - 5}{2}$.
    $\frac{du}{dx} = 2 \implies dx = \frac{1}{2}du$.
2.  Substitute into the integral:
    $$ \int \left(\frac{u - 5}{2}\right) u^{\frac{1}{2}} \left(\frac{1}{2} du\right) $$
    Factor out the constants ($\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$):
    $$ \frac{1}{4} \int (u - 5)u^{\frac{1}{2}} \, du = \frac{1}{4} \int (u^{\frac{3}{2}} - 5u^{\frac{1}{2}}) \, du $$
3.  Integrate with respect to $u$:
    $$ \frac{1}{4} \left( \frac{2}{5}u^{\frac{5}{2}} - \frac{10}{3}u^{\frac{3}{2}} \right) + c = \frac{1}{10}u^{\frac{5}{2}} - \frac{5}{6}u^{\frac{3}{2}} + c $$
4.  Write back in terms of $x$:
    $$ \frac{1}{10}(2x + 5)^{\frac{5}{2}} - \frac{5}{6}(2x + 5)^{\frac{3}{2}} + c $$

*Note on Implicit Substitution:* If a root is involved, it is often tidier to use $u^2 = 2x + 5$. Differentiating implicitly gives $2u \frac{du}{dx} = 2 \implies u \, du = dx$. This avoids fractional powers during the integration step.

---

### 8.6 Integration by Parts

Just as the Product Rule differentiates a product, Integration by Parts integrates a product.

**The Formula:**
$$ \int u \frac{dv}{dx} \, dx = uv - \int v \frac{du}{dx} \, dx $$
Or, more simply: $\int u v' \, dx = uv - \int v u' \, dx$

**How to choose $u$ and $v'$ (LIATE Rule):**
You must choose one part of the product to be $u$ (which you will differentiate to get $u'$) and the other to be $v'$ (which you will integrate to get $v$). 
Choose $u$ based on this priority list:
1.  **L**ogarithmic ($\ln x$)
2.  **I**nverse Trig (Further Maths only)
3.  **A**lgebraic ($x, x^2$)
4.  **T**rigonometric ($\sin x, \cos x$)
5.  **E**xponential ($e^x$)

**Example:** $\int x \cos x \, dx$
*   Let $u = x$ (Algebraic takes priority over Trig). Therefore, $v' = \cos x$.
*   Differentiate $u$: $u' = 1$.
*   Integrate $v'$: $v = \sin x$.
*   Apply formula: $uv - \int v u' \, dx$
    $$ = x \sin x - \int 1 \cdot \sin x \, dx $$
    $$ = x \sin x - (-\cos x) + c = x \sin x + \cos x + c $$

**The Special Case of $\ln x$:**
To integrate $\int \ln x \, dx$, treat it as a product: $\int 1 \cdot \ln x \, dx$.
*   Let $u = \ln x$ and $v' = 1$.
*   $u' = \frac{1}{x}$ and $v = x$.
*   Formula: $x \ln x - \int x \cdot \frac{1}{x} \, dx = x \ln x - \int 1 \, dx = x \ln x - x + c$.

[VISUAL PLACEHOLDER: A21IntegrationSVG-001 | Source: P2 Chapter 11 Integration Slide 31 | Insert from A21_integration_svg.md | Purpose: Visual diagram showing the cross-multiplication pattern for Integration by Parts]

**The DI (Tabular) Method:**
For integrals like $\int x^2 e^x \, dx$, standard Integration by Parts must be applied twice, which is tedious. The DI method is a highly efficient, exam-approved alternative.
1. Create a 'D' (Differentiate) column and an 'I' (Integrate) column.
2. Add alternating signs ($+, -, +, -$) down the side.
3. Put your $u$ term in the D column and differentiate it down to 0.
4. Put your $v'$ term in the I column and integrate it the same number of times.
5. Multiply diagonally and add the terms.

*(See Worked Examples for a full demonstration of the DI method).*

### 8.7 Integrating Fractions

If you have a fractional expression, check the Reverse Chain Rule first. If that fails, look at the denominator:

**1. Single term in the denominator $\rightarrow$ Split the numerator**
$$ \int \frac{x - 1}{\sqrt{x}} \, dx = \int \left( \frac{x}{x^{1/2}} - \frac{1}{x^{1/2}} \right) \, dx = \int (x^{1/2} - x^{-1/2}) \, dx $$

**2. Factorisable denominator $\rightarrow$ Partial Fractions**
$$ \int \frac{10x - 2}{(2x + 1)(x - 3)} \, dx $$
Split into $\frac{A}{2x + 1} + \frac{B}{x - 3}$, find $A=2, B=4$, then integrate to get $\ln$ functions.

**3. Top-heavy (Improper) fraction $\rightarrow$ Algebraic Long Division**
If the highest power of $x$ in the numerator is $\geq$ the highest power in the denominator.
$$ \int \frac{x^2}{x + 1} \, dx $$
Divide $x^2$ by $x+1$ to get $x - 1$ with a remainder of $1$.
$$ \int \left( x - 1 + \frac{1}{x + 1} \right) \, dx = \frac{1}{2}x^2 - x + \ln|x + 1| + c $$

---

### 8.8 Parametric Integration

To find the area under a curve defined parametrically by $x = f(t)$ and $y = g(t)$:
Instead of $\int y \, dx$, we use the chain rule to integrate with respect to $t$:
$$ \text{Area} = \int y \frac{dx}{dt} \, dt $$
*Crucial Step:* You must change the $x$-limits to $t$-limits.

**Example:** $x = t^2$, $y = t + 1$. Area between $x=0$ and $x=3$.
1. $\frac{dx}{dt} = 2t$.
2. Change limits: When $x=3, t=\sqrt{3}$. When $x=0, t=0$.
3. Integrate: $\int_{0}^{\sqrt{3}} (t+1)(2t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{\sqrt{3}} (2t^2 + 2t) \, dt$.

---

### 8.9 The Trapezium Rule

When a function cannot be integrated analytically, we approximate the area under the curve by dividing it into trapeziums of equal width, $h$.

**Formula (given in formula booklet):**
$$ \int_{a}^{b} y \, dx \approx \frac{h}{2} \left( y_0 + 2(y_1 + y_2 + ... + y_{n-1}) + y_n \right) $$
Where $h = \frac{b - a}{n}$ ($n$ is the number of strips).

*   **Overestimate:** If the curve is **convex** ($f''(x) \geq 0$), the straight tops of the trapeziums sit *above* the curve.
*   **Underestimate:** If the curve is **concave** ($f''(x) \leq 0$), the straight tops sit *below* the curve.

### 8.10 Differential Equations

A differential equation contains a derivative (e.g., $\frac{dy}{dx}$). Solving it means finding the original function $y = f(x)$.

**Method: Separation of Variables**
1.  Rearrange the equation to get all $y$ terms on the left with $dy$, and all $x$ terms on the right with $dx$. You can only do this using multiplication and division (factorise first if necessary).
2.  Integrate both sides.
3.  Add a single constant of integration, $+ c$, to the $x$ side.
4.  If asked, rearrange to make $y$ the subject.
5.  If given boundary conditions (e.g., $x=1$ when $y=4$), substitute them in to find the specific value of $c$ (Particular Solution).

*Logarithm Trick:* If integrating gives you $\ln$ terms on both sides, it is highly recommended to write your constant as $\ln k$ or $\ln A$ instead of $c$. This allows you to use log laws to combine terms easily.
Example: $\ln y = \frac{1}{2}\ln(1+x^2) + \ln k \implies \ln y = \ln(k\sqrt{1+x^2}) \implies y = k\sqrt{1+x^2}$.

**Forming Differential Equations from Context:**
Look for keywords:
*   "Rate of increase of population $P$" $\rightarrow \frac{dP}{dt}$
*   "Is proportional to" $\rightarrow = k \times ...$
*   "Inversely proportional to" $\rightarrow = \frac{k}{...}$
*   "Decrease / cooling / flowing out" $\rightarrow$ Use a negative constant, $-k$.

Example: "The rate at which tea cools is proportional to the difference in temperature between the tea ($T$) and the room ($25^\circ C$)."
Equation: $\frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - 25)$.

## 11. Worked Examples

### Example 1: Reverse Chain Rule (Trigonometric)
**Source:** Transcript (Skill #4)
**Question:** Find $\int \cos x \sin^2 x \, dx$
**Method:**
1.  **Consider:** The presence of $\cos x$ alongside a power of $\sin x$ suggests the chain rule was applied to a $\sin$ function. Consider $y = \sin^3 x$.
2.  **Differentiate:** $\frac{dy}{dx} = 3\sin^2 x \cos x$.
3.  **Scale:** The derivative gives us $3\sin^2 x \cos x$, but the integral only asks for $1 \cos x \sin^2 x$. We must scale our considered function by $\frac{1}{3}$.
4.  **Result:** $\int \cos x \sin^2 x \, dx = \frac{1}{3}\sin^3 x + c$

### Example 2: Integration by Substitution
**Source:** Transcript (Skill #5)
**Question:** Find $\int x\sqrt{2x + 5} \, dx$ using the substitution $u = 2x + 5$.
**Method:**
1.  **Differentiate the substitution:** 
    $u = 2x + 5 \implies \frac{du}{dx} = 2 \implies dx = \frac{1}{2}du$
2.  **Rearrange for $x$:**
    $x = \frac{u - 5}{2}$
3.  **Substitute everything into the integral:**
    $\int \left(\frac{u - 5}{2}\right) u^{\frac{1}{2}} \left(\frac{1}{2} du\right)$
4.  **Simplify and factor out constants:**
    $\frac{1}{4} \int (u - 5)u^{\frac{1}{2}} \, du = \frac{1}{4} \int (u^{\frac{3}{2}} - 5u^{\frac{1}{2}}) \, du$
5.  **Integrate with respect to $u$:**
    $\frac{1}{4} \left( \frac{2}{5}u^{\frac{5}{2}} - \frac{10}{3}u^{\frac{3}{2}} \right) + c = \frac{1}{10}u^{\frac{5}{2}} - \frac{5}{6}u^{\frac{3}{2}} + c$
6.  **Write in terms of $x$:**
    $\frac{1}{10}(2x + 5)^{\frac{5}{2}} - \frac{5}{6}(2x + 5)^{\frac{3}{2}} + c$

### Example 3: Integration by Parts (DI / Tabular Method)
**Source:** Transcript (DI Method section)
**Question:** Find $\int x^2 e^{-x} \, dx$
**Method:**
Because there is an $x^2$ term, standard integration by parts would need to be applied twice. The DI method is much faster.
1.  **Set up columns:** D (Differentiate) and I (Integrate), with alternating signs ($+, -, +, -$).
2.  **Populate:**
    *   $(+) \quad x^2 \quad \quad e^{-x}$
    *   $(-) \quad 2x \quad \quad -e^{-x}$
    *   $(+) \quad 2 \quad \quad \quad e^{-x}$
    *   $(-) \quad 0 \quad \quad \quad -e^{-x}$
3.  **Multiply diagonally:**
    *   $(+)(x^2)(-e^{-x}) = -x^2 e^{-x}$
    *   $(-)(2x)(e^{-x}) = -2x e^{-x}$
    *   $(+)(2)(-e^{-x}) = -2e^{-x}$
4.  **Result:** $-x^2 e^{-x} - 2x e^{-x} - 2e^{-x} + c$

### Example 4: Differential Equations with Partial Fractions
**Source:** Transcript (Skill #11)
**Question:** Find the general solution to $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-3(y - 2)}{(2x + 1)(x + 2)}$
**Method:**
1.  **Separate the variables:** Divide by $(y-2)$ and multiply by $dx$.
    $\int \frac{1}{y - 2} \, dy = \int \frac{-3}{(2x + 1)(x + 2)} \, dx$
2.  **Partial Fractions on the RHS:**
    $\frac{-3}{(2x + 1)(x + 2)} \equiv \frac{A}{2x + 1} + \frac{B}{x + 2}$
    $-3 = A(x + 2) + B(2x + 1)$
    Let $x = -2 \implies -3 = B(-3) \implies B = 1$
    Let $x = -0.5 \implies -3 = A(1.5) \implies A = -2$
3.  **Rewrite the integral:**
    $\int \frac{1}{y - 2} \, dy = \int \left( \frac{-2}{2x + 1} + \frac{1}{x + 2} \right) \, dx$
4.  **Integrate both sides:**
    $\ln|y - 2| = -\ln|2x + 1| + \ln|x + 2| + \ln k$
    *(Note the use of $\ln k$ as the constant of integration to make combining logs easier).*
5.  **Combine logs and solve for $y$:**
    $\ln|y - 2| = \ln \left| \frac{k(x + 2)}{2x + 1} \right|$
    $y - 2 = \frac{k(x + 2)}{2x + 1}$
    $y = \frac{k(x + 2)}{2x + 1} + 2$

## 12. Common Mistakes and Exam Traps

*   **Assuming all fractions integrate to $\ln$:** A common trap is seeing $\int \frac{2x}{(x^2 - 3)^2} \, dx$ and writing $\ln(x^2 - 3)^2$. This is incorrect. Because the denominator is squared, you must rewrite it as $2x(x^2 - 3)^{-2}$ and use the reverse chain rule (increase the power to $-1$). It only integrates to $\ln$ if the denominator has a power of $1$.
*   **Forgetting to change limits in Substitution:** When evaluating a definite integral using substitution, you *must* calculate the new $u$-limits. If you use the old $x$-limits on your $u$-integrated function, your answer will be completely wrong.
*   **Integration by Parts sign errors:** The formula is $uv - \int v u' \, dx$. The minus sign before the integral often causes sign errors if $v$ or $u'$ are negative. Always use brackets to protect your signs.
*   **Choosing the wrong $u$ in Parts:** Remember the LIATE rule. If you have $\int x \ln x \, dx$ and you choose $u = x$, you will end up trying to integrate $\ln x$ for $v$, which makes the problem harder, not simpler. $\ln x$ always takes priority for $u$.
*   **Differential Equations - Illegal Separation:** You can only separate variables using multiplication and division. You cannot add or subtract terms to move them across the equals sign if they are attached to the derivative.

## 14. Worked Solutions

**1.** $\int \sec^2 x \, dx = \tan x + c$ (Standard result)

**2.** $\int \frac{x^2}{x^3 + 1} \, dx$
*   Consider $\ln|x^3 + 1|$. Derivative is $\frac{3x^2}{x^3 + 1}$.
*   Scale by $\frac{1}{3}$.
*   Answer: $\frac{1}{3}\ln|x^3 + 1| + c$

**3.** $\int x e^{x^2 + 1} \, dx$
*   Consider $e^{x^2 + 1}$. Derivative is $2x e^{x^2 + 1}$.
*   Scale by $\frac{1}{2}$.
*   Answer: $\frac{1}{2}e^{x^2 + 1} + c$

**4.** $\int \tan^2 x \, dx$
*   Use identity: $\tan^2 x \equiv \sec^2 x - 1$
*   $\int (\sec^2 x - 1) \, dx = \tan x - x + c$

**5.** $\int \sin^2 3x \, dx$
*   Use identity: $\cos 6x = 1 - 2\sin^2 3x \implies \sin^2 3x = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\cos 6x$
*   $\int (\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\cos 6x) \, dx = \frac{1}{2}x - \frac{1}{12}\sin 6x + c$

**6.** $\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \cos x \sqrt{1 + \sin x} \, dx$, $u = 1 + \sin x$
*   $\frac{du}{dx} = \cos x \implies dx = \frac{du}{\cos x}$
*   Limits: $x = \pi/2 \implies u = 1 + \sin(\pi/2) = 2$. $x = 0 \implies u = 1 + \sin(0) = 1$.
*   Substitute: $\int_{1}^{2} \cos x \cdot u^{1/2} \cdot \frac{du}{\cos x} = \int_{1}^{2} u^{1/2} \, du$
*   Integrate: $[\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}]_{1}^{2} = \frac{2}{3}(2)^{3/2} - \frac{2}{3}(1)^{3/2} = \frac{2}{3}(2\sqrt{2} - 1)$

**7.** $\int x \sin 2x \, dx$
*   Parts: $u = x \implies u' = 1$. $v' = \sin 2x \implies v = -\frac{1}{2}\cos 2x$.
*   $uv - \int v u' \, dx = -\frac{1}{2}x \cos 2x - \int -\frac{1}{2}\cos 2x \, dx$
*   $= -\frac{1}{2}x \cos 2x + \frac{1}{4}\sin 2x + c$

**8.** $\int \ln x \, dx$
*   Rewrite as $\int 1 \cdot \ln x \, dx$.
*   Parts: $u = \ln x \implies u' = \frac{1}{x}$. $v' = 1 \implies v = x$.
*   $uv - \int v u' \, dx = x \ln x - \int x(\frac{1}{x}) \, dx = x \ln x - \int 1 \, dx$
*   $= x \ln x - x + c$

**9.** $\frac{dy}{dx} = y(x + 1)$
*   Separate: $\int \frac{1}{y} \, dy = \int (x + 1) \, dx$
*   Integrate: $\ln|y| = \frac{1}{2}x^2 + x + c$
*   Rearrange: $y = e^{\frac{1}{2}x^2 + x + c} = e^c \cdot e^{\frac{1}{2}x^2 + x}$
*   Let $A = e^c \implies y = A e^{\frac{1}{2}x^2 + x}$

## 15. Exam Technique Notes

*   **Always differentiate your answer to check it.** Integration is anti-differentiation. If you have time at the end of an exam, differentiate your integrated expression using the chain, product, or quotient rule. If you don't get the original integrand back, you have made a scaling or sign error.
*   **Show your substitution steps clearly.** Examiners award method marks for finding $\frac{du}{dx}$, substituting it in, and explicitly showing the calculation for the new limits. Do not do this entirely in your head or on your calculator.
*   **Use your calculator to check definite integrals.** Most modern A-Level calculators have a definite integral button. Use it to check your final numerical answer, but remember you must show the algebraic integration steps to get the marks.
*   **Don't force a complex method.** If a fraction's numerator is the exact derivative of the denominator, it is a 1-mark reverse chain rule question ($\ln|f(x)|$). Do not waste 10 minutes doing a formal substitution.
