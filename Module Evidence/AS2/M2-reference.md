## 5. Key Vocabulary

Position Vector: Location of a particle relative to a fixed origin 6.

Displacement: Directed change in position from a starting point (vector) 6.

Distance: Total length of the path travelled (scalar) 6.

Velocity: Rate of change of displacement (vector) 6.

Speed: Rate of change of distance, or the magnitude of velocity (scalar) 6.

Acceleration: Rate of change of velocity (vector) 6.

Deceleration: A negative acceleration relative to the direction of motion, indicating the object is slowing down 6.

## 7. Full Theory ExplanationLanguage of KinematicsKinematics is the study of motion 7. It relies on a precise mathematical language 7. Distance and speed are scalar quantities, meaning they have magnitude but no direction 7. Displacement and velocity are vectors, meaning they have both magnitude and direction 7. For example, if a cyclist travels 5 km away and 5 km back, her total distance is 10 km, but her final displacement is 0 km 7.

Motion Graphs

Displacement-time graphs: The gradient tells us how quickly displacement is changing 8. Therefore, the gradient gives the velocity 8. A flat horizontal line means the object is stationary 8. A straight sloping line means constant velocity 8.

Velocity-time graphs: The gradient gives the acceleration 8. A flat horizontal line above the axis means the object is moving at a constant velocity (zero acceleration) 8. The area under a velocity-time graph gives the displacement (and distance, provided the velocity remains positive) 8.

Exam-Style Note: When asked to describe the motion of an athlete or particle between two times from a graph, ensure you give enough detail (such as explicitly stating "constant acceleration" or calculating the exact acceleration value) to gain the mark 8, 9. Examiners note that candidates often fail to provide enough detail for these descriptions 8, 9.

Constant Acceleration (SUVAT)When an object is moving in a straight line with constant acceleration, its motion is governed by the five SUVAT equations 10. The variables are:

$s = \text{displacement}$ 10

$u = \text{initial velocity}$ 10

$v = \text{final velocity}$ 10

$a = \text{acceleration}$ 10

$t = \text{time}$ 10

You must define a positive direction before substituting values 10. If a particle is decelerating while moving in the positive direction, its acceleration $a$ will be negative 10.

Vertical Motion Under GravityVertical motion is a standard constant acceleration topic 10. If we ignore air resistance, an object in freefall accelerates downwards at $g = 9.8 \text{ m s}^{-2}$ regardless of its mass 10. You must pick a positive direction 10. If upwards is positive, $a = -9.8$ 10. If downwards is positive, $a = 9.8$ 10. At the maximum height of a vertical projection, the particle is instantaneously at rest, meaning $v = 0$ 10.

Constant Acceleration in Two Dimensions (Vectors)In 2D kinematics, displacement ($\mathbf{s}$), initial velocity ($\mathbf{u}$), final velocity ($\mathbf{v}$), and acceleration ($\mathbf{a}$) become vectors, while time ($t$) remains a scalar 11. You apply the SUVAT formulae component-by-component 11. For example, the equation $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{a}t$ applies to the $\mathbf{i}$ components and the $\mathbf{j}$ components simultaneously 11.

The position vector $\mathbf{r}$ of a particle is given by its initial position $\mathbf{r}_0$ plus its displacement $\mathbf{s}$, giving $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{s}$ 11.

## 8. Formulae and Notation

Area of a trapezium: $A = \frac{1}{2}(a+b)h$ 12

SUVAT Equations (1D & 2D Vectors): 12

$\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{a}t$ 12

$\mathbf{s} = \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v})t$ 12

$\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{u}t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2$ 12

$\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{v}t - \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2$ (This is a perfectly acceptable formula to use in the CCEA exam) 12, 13.

$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ (1D scalar motion only) 12

Position Vector: $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{u}t + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}t^2$ 12

Speed: $|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$ 12

## 9. Step-by-Step Methods

### Method A: Velocity-Time Graph Problem Solving

Sketch the graph from the motion description, marking known times and velocities on the axes 14.

Identify the shape (usually a triangle or trapezium) 14.

Set the area of the shape equal to the total distance travelled 14.

Solve the resulting equation algebraically to find the unknown time or velocity 14.

Exam Insight: Take extreme care with your algebraic manipulation. Examiners have reported that while candidates know how to approach these questions, many struggle with the numerical and algebraic aspects of finding the area under the graph to successfully isolate unknown time $T$ 9, 14.

### Method B: 1D SUVAT and Gravity Setup

Draw a quick sketch. Mark the positive direction clearly 14.

List the variables $s, u, v, a, t$. Fill in the knowns (use $a = \pm 9.8$ for gravity) and put a question mark next to the target variable 14.

Choose the SUVAT formula that contains your three knowns and one target variable 14.

Substitute, solve, and include units 14.

Check: If a quadratic gives a negative time, reject it if it occurs before the physical start of the journey 14.

### Method C: 2D Vector SUVAT

Write all given vectors in $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}$ or column vector form 15.

Select the vector SUVAT formula 15.

Treat $t$ as a scalar that scales both the horizontal and vertical components of velocity or acceleration 15.

Add the $\mathbf{i}$ components separately from the $\mathbf{j}$ components 15.

To find speed, use Pythagoras on the final velocity components 15.

Critical Exam Step: If the question specifically asks for the distance between two points (e.g., "Find the distance PQ"), you must complete the question by finding the magnitude of your final displacement vector using Pythagoras 15. A large number of candidates fail to complete the question by skipping this magnitude step 15, 16.

## 10. Worked Examples with Full Written Working

Example 1: Velocity-Time Graph Area Exam-StyleA car travels along a straight horizontal road between two sets of traffic lights, a distance of 1500 m 15. It accelerates uniformly from rest to a speed $V \text{ m s}^{-1}$, then immediately decelerates uniformly to rest at the second set of lights 15. The journey takes 120 seconds. Find $V$ 15.

Step 1: Model the journey as a triangle on a $v-t$ graph. The base is 120, and the height is $V$ 17.

Step 2: Set area = distance 17.$\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$ 17$1500 = \frac{1}{2}(120)V$ 17

Step 3: Solve for V 17.$1500 = 60V$ 17$V = 25 \text{ m s}^{-1}$ 17

### Example 2: Vertical Motion Exam-StyleA ball is projected vertically upwards from a point 7 m above the ground with an initial speed of 21 m s$^{-1}$ 17. Find the greatest height above the ground reached by the ball 17.

Step 1: Choose downwards as positive 18.$u = -21$ (because it moves up) 18$a = 9.8$ 18$v = 0$ (at greatest height) 18

Step 2: Choose formula. We want $s$ 18.$v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ 18$0 = (-21)^2 + 2(9.8)s$ 18$0 = 441 + 19.6s$ 18$19.6s = -441 \Rightarrow s = -22.5 \text{ m}$ 18

Step 3: Interpret $s$. It rose 22.5 m above the launch point 18.$\text{Height above ground} = 22.5 + 7 = 29.5 \text{ m}$ 18.

Example 3: 2D Vector Velocity Targeted PracticeA particle has initial velocity $\mathbf{u} = 3\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j} \text{ m s}^{-1}$ and constant acceleration $\mathbf{a} = 2\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j} \text{ m s}^{-2}$ 18. Find its velocity after 5 seconds 18.

Step 1: Use $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{a}t$ 19.

Step 2: Substitute and scale 19.$\mathbf{v} = (3\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j}) + (2\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j})(5)$ 19$\mathbf{v} = 3\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j} + 10\mathbf{i} + 20\mathbf{j}$ 19

Step 3: Group components 19.$\mathbf{v} = 13\mathbf{i} + 19\mathbf{j} \text{ m s}^{-1}$ 19.

## 12. Common Traps

Multiple Conflicting Solutions: In exams, if you offer two solutions (e.g., one where gravity is positive and one where it is negative) and do not cross one out, the examiner will award the poorest mark 22, 23. Choose a sign convention and stick to it 23. CCEA explicitly states that for multiple responses where candidates are clearly trying to cover all options without making a selection, the poorer mark will be automatically awarded 24, 25.

Distance vs Displacement Magnitude Trap: When a question asks for distance in a 2D vector kinematics scenario, finding the displacement vector (e.g., $14\mathbf{i} + \mathbf{j}$) is not enough 23. You must calculate the magnitude of the displacement vector using Pythagoras 23. Failing to finish the calculation is a heavily documented examiner penalty 9, 16, 23.

Squaring Vectors: Never write $\mathbf{v}^2 = \mathbf{u}^2 + 2\mathbf{a}\mathbf{s}$ 23. Vectors cannot be squared directly in AS2 SUVAT 23. The equation $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ applies strictly to 1D scalar kinematics or individual scalar components (like $v_x^2 = u_x^2 + 2a_x s_x$) 23.

Confusing $s$ for speed: In SUVAT, $s$ means displacement, not speed 23. Speed is the scalar magnitude of velocity 23.

Reversing Direction: When an object decelerates uniformly, it can eventually come to rest and then accelerate in the reverse direction (yielding a negative velocity) 23.

Final Impact Speed: When a question asks for the speed a falling object "strikes the floor", it means the instantaneous speed exactly at impact 23. Do not say the speed is $0 \text{ m s}^{-1}$ because it eventually stops 23.

## 13. Calculator Validation

The exam strictly prohibits calculators with Computer Algebra Systems (CAS) 26, 27. However, solving quadratic equations using a standard polynomial solver is perfectly acceptable to save time in mechanics, provided you first write down the full quadratic equation (e.g., $4.9t^2 - 21t - 7 = 0$) 27, 28. Use the solver to validate your arithmetic, but it does not replace the requirement to show your initial equation formulation 27.

## 14. Exam Technique

Time Management: Because the AS2 paper progresses in difficulty within each section, you should take extreme care with your time management 29. Allocate equal time to Section A (Mechanics) and Section B (Statistics) 29.

Accuracy and Careful Reading: Small numerical errors are incredibly common in vector kinematics questions. Always double-check your $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$ coefficients against the original question text to prevent throwing away easy marks 16.

Rounding: In mechanics, decimal answers are highly preferred over exact surds 27. Where rounding is required, the final answer must be given to 3 significant figures 22, 27.

Intermediate Stages: To guarantee final accuracy, you must keep more than 3 significant figures during intermediate calculations. Only round at the very end 22, 27.

Units: Always state final units 27. In multi-part vector kinematics questions, remember to attach $\text{m s}^{-1}$, $\text{m s}^{-2}$, or $\text{m}$ to your final vector answer 27.

> **⚠️ EXAM WARNING: THE "MULTIPLE RESPONSES" RULEA common pitfall for high-achieving candidates is the tendency to provide multiple solutions to a difficult problem in the hope that the examiner will select the correct one 30. Within the CCEA regulatory framework, this practice is actively penalized 30.**

The Rule of the "Poorest Attempt": The CCEA marking policy for mathematics is explicit regarding multiple uncrossed responses. If you provide two or more different methods or answers to the same question without clearly crossing one out, and are clearly trying to cover all options, the examiner is instructed to mark the poorest attempt 24, 25, 30.

Strategic Decision Making: This rule applies heavily to kinematics. If you offer 2 solutions for motion under gravity with opposite signs for $g$ but do not choose a final solution, the poorer mark is awarded 22. If you attempt to solve a kinematics problem using both constant acceleration formulas and variable acceleration (integration), and both are left on the page, the poorer method is marked 31.

CRITICAL WARNING: If you have made a mistake or attempted a question using an alternative method, you MUST decisively cross out the version you do not want to be marked 25, 32. Do not "hedge your bets." Mistakes that are crossed out will not be penalized; mistakes that are left on the page alongside correct work will cost you marks 32.

## 19. End-of-Lesson Summary

The foundation of kinematics relies heavily on matching the physical situation to the correct mathematical model 37. Graphing motion allows us to instantly visualize journey stages, using gradients for rates of change and area for displacement 37. When acceleration is constant, the SUVAT formulae efficiently link the five key kinematic quantities 37. Crucially, these same scalar principles extend directly to two-dimensional motion by treating the $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$ components completely independently 37.
