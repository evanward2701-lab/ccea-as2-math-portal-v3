## 5. Key Vocabulary

Population: The whole group of individuals or items being investigated.

Sample: A smaller group taken from the population to estimate its characteristics.

Census: Data collected from every single member of the population.

Sampling Frame: A complete numbered list of all members of the population used to draw a sample.

Simple Random Sample: A sample where every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected.

Stratified Sample: A sample that divides the population into distinct groups (strata) and selects a random sample from each group proportionally.

Qualitative Data: Non-numerical category data (e.g., colour, transport method).

Quantitative Data: Numerical data.

Discrete Data: Quantitative data that can only take specific, usually counted, values (e.g., number of students).

Continuous Data: Quantitative data that can take any value within a range, usually measured (e.g., time, height).

Bias: A systematic unfairness in the way data is collected, meaning the sample is not representative of the population.

## 7. Full Theory Explanation

Populations, Samples, and the CensusA statistical investigation begins by defining the target population. This is the entire group of interest (e.g., "all students at a specific college").A census occurs when you collect data from every single member of this population. While a census is highly accurate, it is often too slow, expensive, or impractical to carry out.Instead, we usually take a sample—a smaller group selected from the population. We use the sample to make an informal inference (an educated estimate) about the whole population. Because we are only looking at a fraction of the whole, different samples can lead to different conclusions due to natural sampling variation or bias.

Data TypesOnce you have your sample, you collect data. This data falls into clear categories:

Qualitative (Categorical): Describes qualities or categories (e.g., favourite subject, make of car).

Quantitative (Numerical): Represents quantities. This splits into two sub-types:

Discrete: Counted values that jump in steps (e.g., number of siblings, shoe size).

Continuous: Measured values that can be any decimal on a continuous scale (e.g., weight in kg, time taken in seconds).

Sampling MethodsAS2 focuses on two main random sampling methods:

Simple Random Sampling: To do this, you need a sampling frame (a list of the entire population). You number everyone on the list and use a random number generator (or a calculator) to select the sample. Every item has an equal chance of selection. This removes human bias but does not guarantee the sample will perfectly mirror the population structure.Note from CCEA AS2 Exam Support: Candidates should be aware of how to use random number tables or a calculator when finding a random sample. However, you are unlikely to be asked to physically carry this out in an exam. You must instead be able to clearly describe and explain the theoretical process.

Stratified Sampling: The population is divided into distinct, non-overlapping groups (called strata), such as year groups or departments. A random sample is then drawn from each stratum in exact proportion to the size of that group in the population. This ensures the final sample is highly representative of the population's structure.

Bias and Critiquing SamplesAn unrepresentative sample leads to biased results. A biased sample systematically favours some responses over others. For example, if you want to know the average hours students spend studying, selecting a sample of "students in the library after school" introduces bias. Those students are more likely to study longer hours than the general population.Note from CCEA AS2 Exam Support: Selecting or critiquing data presentation and sampling techniques in the context of a statistical problem is frequently tested. The examiner guidance notes this requires a simple explanation, similar to GCSE level, but it must be clearly and explicitly linked to the specific real-world scenario provided in the question.

## 8. Formulae and Notation

Proportional Stratified Sample Size Formula:$$\text{Stratum sample size} = \frac{\text{Stratum population size}}{\text{Total population size}} \times \text{Overall sample size}$$

## 9. Step-by-Step Methods

### Method: Describing a Simple Random Sample

Define a complete sampling frame (e.g., obtain a numbered list of all members of the target population).

Number every item/person in the sampling frame from $1$ to $N$.

Use a calculator, computer, or random number table to generate random numbers.

Select the corresponding members for the sample. Ignore any repeated numbers or any numbers generated that fall outside the target range.

### Method: Calculating Stratified Sample Sizes

Identify the total population size by adding all the groups together (if not given).

Identify the target overall sample size.

For each group (stratum), set up the fraction: (Group Size / Total Population).

Multiply this fraction by the overall sample size.

If the result is a decimal, round to the nearest whole number.

Check: Add your rounded stratum sizes together to ensure they equal the exact overall sample size requested.

### Method: Critiquing a Sampling Procedure

Identify the target population.

Identify how the sample was collected.

Ask: "Who is left out?" or "Who is over-represented?"

Write your conclusion heavily linked to the specific context of the question (e.g., do not just write "it is biased", write "early arrivals may have different travel habits than late arrivals").

## 10. Worked Examples with Full Written Working

Example 1: Stratified Sampling Targeted PracticeA college has 300 students: 180 study Mathematics and 120 do not. A sample of 40 students is needed, stratified by whether they study Mathematics. Find how many of each group should be selected.

Step 1: Identify totals. Total population = 300. Target sample = 40.

Step 2: Calculate for Mathematics students.$\text{Maths sample} = \frac{180}{300} \times 40$$= 0.6 \times 40 = 24$

Step 3: Calculate for non-Mathematics students.$\text{Non-Maths sample} = \frac{120}{300} \times 40$$= 0.4 \times 40 = 16$

Step 4: Check.$24 + 16 = 40$. The sample sizes are correct.

Example 2: Data Types and Bias Targeted PracticeA school wants to estimate the mean number of hours per week its 500 sixth-form students spend on independent study. The school surveys 30 students attending an after-school study club.(a) State the data type for "hours per week".(b) Explain why this sample may lead to an incorrect conclusion about the population.

Solution (a): Time is measured, so it is quantitative continuous data.

Solution (b): The sample is biased because it was taken exclusively from an after-school study club. These students are likely to spend more time studying than the general sixth-form population, so the sample will over-estimate the true mean.

## 12. Common Traps

Vague definition of a Census: V1 examiner reports note that "the definition of a census often lacked detail". You must state it is data collected from every single member of the population. Just saying "asking everyone" is too vague.

Ignoring Context in Bias: When asked why a sample is bad, writing "it is biased" will score zero marks. You must state why in context (e.g., "People at the hospital on a Monday morning do not represent the opinions of people who work standard 9-5 jobs").

Rounding errors in Stratified Sampling: If you calculate stratum sizes and round them, you must add them up manually at the end. Sometimes standard rounding causes the total to be 1 person over or 1 person under the required sample size.

Confusing Discrete and Continuous: Money and age are technically continuous (time flows continuously, money can be fractions of a penny in banking), but in AS2 data presentation questions, they are often grouped into discrete-style bins. If a value is measured using an instrument (stopwatch, ruler, scales), it is continuous.

Making Inferences: If an exam question asks you to infer something about a population from a sample, apply the sample's percentage/proportion directly to the population total.

Context is King: Examiner reports explicitly state that sampling questions provide "a suitable opportunity for candidates to accurately display their understanding of sampling bias in a contextual question". Marks W1 are strictly withheld if your answer is generic and does not name the variables in the prompt.

Question 2 Exam-StyleA market researcher is interested to find out the opinions of nurses living in Northern Ireland. The organiser wants a stratified sample by year group.(a) Find the number of AS students and A2 students that should be selected.(b) State whether travel time is qualitative, quantitative discrete, or quantitative continuous data.(c) Give one reason why a stratified sample may be better than asking the first 50 students who arrive at school.

## 16. Full Worked Solutions

Question 1(a) A census collects data from every single member of the population MW1, whereas a sample collects data from only a selected smaller group (or subset) of the population MW1.(b) A census is usually very time-consuming (or expensive/difficult to process) W1.

### Question 2Any two valid contextual points:

He is only questioning women, ignoring any male nurses living in Northern Ireland W1.

By sampling only at the Royal Victoria Hospital, he is excluding nurses who work at other hospitals or clinics across Northern Ireland W1.

By questioning people on a Monday morning after 9 am, he misses nurses who work night shifts or are on leave W1.

He assumes everyone entering the hospital is a nurse, which is incorrect (could be patients/visitors) W1.

Question 3(a) Total population = $120 + 80 = 200$. M1AS students: $\frac{120}{200} \times 50 = 30$ A1A2 students: $\frac{80}{200} \times 50 = 20$ A1(b) Travel time is measured, so it is quantitative continuous data. W1(c) A stratified sample guarantees the correct proportional representation of AS and A2 students. Asking the first 50 arrivals is biased because early arrivals might have different travel times/patterns than those who arrive later. W1

## 17. Final Answers

Q1: (a) Census = all members; sample = subset. (b) Expensive/time-consuming.

Q2: Ignores male nurses, ignores other hospitals, ignores night-shift workers.

Q3: (a) 30 AS, 20 A2. (b) Quantitative continuous. (c) Maintains population proportions; first 50 is biased towards early risers.

## 19. End-of-Lesson SummaryStatistical sampling is the foundation of all data analysis. Because a census is rarely practical, we rely on samples to make informal inferences about massive populations.
