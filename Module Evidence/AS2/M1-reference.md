## 5. Key Vocabulary

Fundamental Quantity: A base physical measurement (e.g., mass, time, length) 2.

Derived Quantity: A measurement built from base units (e.g., acceleration, force) 2.

Scalar: A quantity with magnitude (size) only 2.

Vector: A quantity with both magnitude and direction 2.

Particle: A modelling assumption where an object's dimensions are negligible and its mass is concentrated at a single point 2.

Inextensible: A string or cable that does not stretch under load 2.

Tension: A pulling force transmitted through a string or cable 2.

Normal Reaction: A contact force acting perpendicular to a surface 2.

## 7. Full Theory Explanation

Mechanics OverviewMechanics focuses on two main ideas: Forces and Motion 3. These two branches are permanently linked by Newton’s Second Law ($F=ma$), meaning that a resultant force acting on a mass causes it to accelerate 3.

SI Units and Derived UnitsTo standardise calculations, mechanics relies on the Système International (SI) units 4. You must convert measurements into SI base units before beginning any calculation 4.

Mass is measured in kilograms (kg) 4.

Length/Displacement is measured in metres (m) 4.

Time is measured in seconds (s) 4.

Derived units are created by combining these base units:

Velocity is measured in metres per second (m s$^{-1}$) 4.

Acceleration measures the change in velocity per second, giving metres per second squared (m s$^{-2}$) 4. The notation $\text{ms}^{-2}$ is perfectly acceptable in exams 4.

Force is measured in newtons (N). Because $F = ma$, one newton is equal to one kilogram multiplied by one metre per second squared ($1 \text{ N} = 1 \text{ kg m s}^{-2}$) 4.

Exam Context: CCEA examiners often test unit conversion natively by embedding non-SI units into standard questions, such as providing mass in tonnes in an $F=ma$ exercise 5. This requires immediate conversion before formula substitution.

Mass versus WeightMass is a scalar measure of the amount of matter in an object, measured in kg 6. Weight is a force caused by the pull of gravity acting vertically downwards on that mass 6. Because it is a force, weight is measured in newtons and is calculated using $W = mg$, where $g = 9.8 \text{ m s}^{-2}$ 6.

Scalars and VectorsIn mechanics, quantities are grouped into two categories:

Scalars have magnitude (size) only. Examples include distance, speed, time, and mass 6.

Vectors have both magnitude and direction. Examples include displacement, velocity, acceleration, and force 6.

Modelling AssumptionsMathematical models simplify complex real-world situations 7. In mechanics, specific words in an exam question act as strict mathematical rules:

Particle: The object's dimensions are negligible. Its mass acts at a single point, and rotational effects or air resistance can be ignored 7.

Smooth surface: There is no friction present 7.

Rough surface: Friction is present and will oppose any attempted motion 7.

Light string/pulley: The string or pulley has no mass. For strings, this means tension is equal throughout 7.

Inextensible string: The string does not stretch. If two objects are connected by it, they will share the exact same magnitude of acceleration 7.

Smooth pulley: There is no friction in the pulley, ensuring tension is the same on both sides of the string 7.

Rod: The object is rigid and does not bend or buckle 7.

## 8. Formulae and Notation

Weight: $W = mg$ (using $g = 9.8 \text{ m s}^{-2}$ unless otherwise stated) 8.

Force Unit Derivation: $1 \text{ N} \equiv 1 \text{ kg m s}^{-2}$ 8.

Magnitude of a 2D Vector: For a vector $\mathbf{v} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j}$ or $\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$, the magnitude is $|\mathbf{v}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ 8.

## 9. Step-by-Step Methods

### Method: Converting a 2D Vector into a Scalar Magnitude

Identify the $x$ and $y$ (or $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$) components of the vector 8.

Square both components using Pythagoras. Ensure negative components are placed in brackets so they yield a positive square 8.

Add the squared values and take the square root to find the magnitude ($|v| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$) 8.

Attach the correct SI unit (e.g., m s$^{-1}$ for speed, N for force) 8.

## 10. Worked Examples with Full Written Working

### Example 1: Unit Conversion and WeightA machine has a mass of $3.5$ tonnes. Calculate its weight.

Step 1: Convert to SI units. Mass $= 3.5 \times 1000 = 3500 \text{ kg}$

Step 2: Apply the weight formula. $W = mg$$W = 3500 \times 9.8$$W = 34300 \text{ N}$

### Example 2: Vector MagnitudeA particle's velocity is modelled by the vector $\mathbf{v} = 6\mathbf{i} - 8\mathbf{j} \text{ m s}^{-1}$. Find its speed.

Step 1: Recognise speed is the scalar magnitude of velocity. Speed $= \sqrt{6^2 + (-8)^2}$

Step 2: Calculate. Speed $= \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100}$Speed $= 10 \text{ m s}^{-1}$

## 12. Common Traps

Leaving answers as surds: Unlike pure mathematics, mechanics heavily relies on decimal answers in context. Leave answers to 3 significant figures unless an exact surd is cleaner or specified 10.

Forgetting to convert mass: The exam frequently tests units by giving mass in tonnes. You must convert to kilograms before using $F=ma$ or $W=mg$ 10.

Confusing mass and weight: Always use $W = mg$ to find the force downwards. Do not label a force diagram with just "5 kg"; it must be "$5g$" 10.

Using the wrong sign in 1D vectors: Left and Down are generally negative. Forgetting to assign negative signs to opposite vectors will ruin magnitude and displacement equations 10.

Hedging your bets: Leaving two distinct mathematical methods on the page because you aren't sure which one is correct. Examiners are forced to penalize this (see Exam Technique below) 11, 12.

## 13. Calculator Validation

Calculators are vital for converting units and safely computing Pythagorean magnitudes. When calculating $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, you must use brackets on your calculator for any negative components (e.g., (-4)^2) to prevent the calculator from subtracting the squared value 13. Note that a calculator checks arithmetic, but it does not replace the requirement to show your written method. Additionally, students must not have access to technology with a computer algebra system (CAS) function during CCEA examinations 14.

## 14. Exam Technique

Rounding: Where rounding is required, your final answer should be given to 3 significant figures 13. To guarantee final accuracy, you must keep more than 3 significant figures during the intermediate stages of a multi-part question 13.

Multiple Responses: If you are unsure of a sign (e.g., gravity direction) and offer two solutions on the page without crossing one out, the examiner will award marks for the poorest solution. You must make a clear choice 13.

Units: Every final answer in a mechanics question must include its correct SI unit 13.

> **⚠️ EXAM WARNING: THE "MULTIPLE RESPONSES" RULEIf you are unsure of how to tackle a problem and you provide two different methods or answers on your page in the hope that the examiner will select the correct one, you will be heavily penalised 15. Official CCEA examiner guidance dictates that if a candidate provides multiple responses clearly trying to cover all options without crossing one out, the examiner will mark all attempts and award the marks for the POOREST solution 12, 16. If you restart a question or change your mind about a mathematical model, you must decisively cross out the incorrect attempt with a single diagonal line so that only your intended, final solution remains active 16, 17.**
